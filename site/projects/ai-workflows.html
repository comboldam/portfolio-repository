Completion needs something observable.

Working with AI assistants has made me attentive to the gap between a reported outcome and a demonstrated one. A confident summary can be useful, but it still leaves a practical question: does the system behave as requested?

My independent prototyping explores that question through everyday development workflows. The focus is on making tasks understandable, reviewing their outcomes, and using failures to improve the next attempt.

I define, inspect, and redirect.

I specify requirements, set priorities, and define acceptance criteria. After an attempted change, I compare reported completion with observed behavior and document problems that require further work.

AI agents support implementation and technical test execution. My contribution is product and workflow direction: deciding what should happen, inspecting whether it did, and directing corrective work when the result falls short.

This division of work makes clear requirements especially valuable. The quality of a result depends partly on whether the desired behavior has been expressed precisely enough to check.

Make the next check easier.

I approach a task by identifying the behavior that would count as success. A useful completion criterion describes an outcome that can be inspected, rather than only the actions an assistant intends to take.

During review, I separate the plan, the attempted work, the reported result, and the behavior I can observe. When those disagree, the mismatch becomes a starting point for correction.

The guiding question

What evidence would make this result understandable to someone who did not watch the work happen?

Failure notes matter because they preserve what went wrong and what needs checking next time. I am interested in turning those observations into clearer requirements and more useful review practices.

What should a progress report prove?

Consider a task-status view. A label such as “working” is useful only if it corresponds to current work. A percentage also needs a basis: what is complete, what remains, and which evidence supports the estimate?

The following is an illustrative acceptance checklist. It explains the kind of review I want to make repeatable; it is not a report of a completed benchmark.

An example of turning a request into checks
QuestionWhat I would inspect
Is the status current?A visible update time and behavior when fresh data is unavailable.
Is progress meaningful?Completed deliverables and remaining steps behind the displayed estimate.
Can work continue?A specific blocker, the next action, and who needs to act.

This example captures a broader design problem: the person supervising an assistant needs enough information to decide whether to wait, inspect, or intervene. A visually polished status display cannot answer that question unless its underlying claims can be checked.

From practical lessons to evaluation.

This work is an ongoing exploration. It has not yet produced a controlled benchmark or a validated estimate of time saved. The current contribution is the practice of defining tasks, reviewing outcomes, and documenting corrections.

The next question is how to evaluate that practice systematically. I want to understand which checks reduce repeated errors, which require too much human effort, and how a workflow can communicate uncertainty without becoming difficult to use.

A bounded next experiment

I would start with a small set of comparable tasks and define success before execution. Alongside the final result, I would record human corrections, repeated failures, and review time. A more detailed report would count as an improvement only if it helped someone reach or verify a useful outcome with reasonable effort.

These questions connect AI-assisted building with my broader interest in evaluation: a process should be judged by what it helps someone accomplish and by the evidence available to assess it.
