Give a small session a clear shape.

Quizzena began with an interest in making knowledge practice feel approachable. The product centers on a simple sequence: choose a topic, answer questions, receive feedback, and return to a record of progress.

The challenge was turning that idea into consistent behavior. Quiz rules, progression, achievements, and navigation all needed to make sense together. Each feature also needed a clear definition before it could be implemented and checked.

My contribution: direction through delivery.

I conceived the product, defined its requirements, and directed AI-assisted development. My work included deciding how play and progression should work, reviewing the resulting behavior, and prioritizing corrections.

AI tools supported implementation. I tested the app on my Android device, compared what I observed with the intended behavior, and directed revisions. Taking responsibility for those decisions was central to getting the product into a releasable form.

  • Product rules: translate the idea into quiz modes, feedback, and progression requirements.
  • Iteration: inspect the experience, identify mismatches, and decide what needs attention next.
  • Release: carry the project through testing and the Android publication process.

A round, a response, a reason to return.

The experience brings together three different jobs. Topic selection gives a session a subject. Answer feedback makes each attempt understandable. Progression connects one short session to the next. Keeping those jobs distinct helps me describe what a feature should accomplish before asking for implementation.

How I frame the product for review
Part of the experienceA useful review question
Topics & quiz modesCan a player understand what they are choosing and how the round works?
Answer feedbackIs the result of each attempt clear enough to guide the next one?
XP & achievementsDoes the recorded progress correspond to what happened during play?

These are examples of review questions, rather than a claim that every part has been formally usability-tested. They show how I translate a broad idea into behavior that can be inspected.

A product people can try.

Quizzena is available on Google Play. Its released experience includes topic-based quizzes, immediate answer feedback, XP and levels, streaks, and achievements. Classic, Time Attack, and 3 Hearts modes offer different ways to play.

The current public release focuses on solo play and progression. The store listing provides an external, inspectable record of what the app offers. Rankings are described there as a future feature.

The concrete outcome

A product idea became an Android app that someone else can install and use.

The release demonstrates delivery: defining a product, working through revisions, and making it available outside my own device. It does not, by itself, demonstrate adoption, retention, or educational impact. Those require separate evidence.

Engagement opens a research question.

Building Quizzena sharpened my interest in learning evaluation. XP, streaks, and correct answers can describe activity inside a product. They do not, on their own, establish durable understanding.

I want to investigate what users remember later and whether they can apply what they learn to unfamiliar questions. That remains a future evaluation direction; I have not established a learning effect.

A possible next study

A small pilot could define a specific body of knowledge, measure what participants know before practice, and test them again after a delay. Including unfamiliar questions would help distinguish recall of a particular answer from broader understanding. A suitable comparison condition would be needed before attributing any improvement to Quizzena.

This distinction connects the app to my graduate studies: creating an experience gives me something concrete to examine, while evaluation asks what evidence would justify a claim about its value.
