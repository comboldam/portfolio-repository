Move from a platform to an outcome.

For my quantitative methods coursework at Rice University, I developed a preliminary proposal around ASSISTments, an educational platform for mathematics practice and feedback.

The question is whether using ASSISTments together with teacher professional learning improves math achievement among students in grades 6–8. Framing the intervention as a combined program matters: an evaluation of that package would not automatically isolate the contribution of the software alone.

The proposal identifies external MAP math assessment outcomes as a possible measure of achievement. That makes the intended outcome distinct from activity recorded inside the platform.

Build the comparison before estimating an effect.

The proposed approach combines matching with difference-in-differences. Matching would seek a comparison group with similar observed characteristics. Difference-in-differences would compare how outcomes change over time between participating and comparison groups.

The design is intended to address a central difficulty: students and schools that adopt an educational tool may already differ from those that do not. A difference in scores after adoption alone would not resolve that problem.

The key assumption

The groups need a credible basis for comparison, including evidence relevant to how their outcomes would have changed without the program.

Pre-intervention trends, the timing of participation, and other changes occurring alongside the program would all affect how convincing that comparison could be.

What matching can and cannot address

I would use characteristics measured before participation to assess whether the groups are comparable. Matching on those characteristics would not remove every possible difference, especially differences that are unobserved. It would also be important to check whether suitable comparison observations exist rather than force poor matches.

Similar earlier score patterns would support the case for the design, but would not prove that the groups would have followed parallel trends without the program. School changes, participation timing, and differences in who remains in the data would still need attention.

Let feasibility shape the analysis.

Before analysis could proceed, I would need to inspect the data and confirm that the required fields are available and usable.

Access to a suitable dataset and the presence of these fields remain unverified. The eventual design would need to follow what the evidence supports.

A public dataset containing platform activity would not necessarily answer this question. I would need to establish whether it can be linked to the intended external outcome, the relevant time periods, and a defensible comparison group. Missing assessment records and changing student samples would also need to be described before interpreting results.

A proposal that makes the next step clear.

The current output is a preliminary course proposal. I have not completed an analysis or produced a causal estimate.

Next decisions, in order

  • Establish feasibility: locate a suitable source, inspect its documentation, and confirm access to the fields the question requires.
  • Specify the comparison: define participation, the observation period, baseline measures, and the level at which the program is introduced.
  • Assess the design: examine comparability, earlier outcome patterns, and missingness before deciding whether an effect estimate would be credible.

If the available data cannot support those steps, the responsible next move is to narrow the question or revise the design. Choosing a method does not make the needed evidence available.

The exercise has helped me articulate a question, connect it to an outcome, and identify what would need to be true for the proposed method to be useful. It also brings my product interests into conversation with evaluation: a promising learning experience creates a question that requires a credible comparison to answer.
